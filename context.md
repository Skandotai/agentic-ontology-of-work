# Context: why this ontology exists

The short version of the argument. For the full paper, see the [whitepaper](whitepaper.md), also available as a [PDF](docs/downloads/agentic-ontology-of-work-v2.0.pdf).

## Vocabulary was step one

The [SOA-to-Agentic AI Terminology Mapping](https://github.com/Skandotai/soa-to-agentic-terms) borrowed a vocabulary the industry had already agreed on once, Service-Oriented Architecture's, and translated it into agentic terms. That gives people the same words. It doesn't yet give them the same structure: two teams can agree on what an "Agent" and an "Intent" are and still model the link between them differently, and then their systems still can't share a record of who did what, or why.

SOA hit the same wall and solved it the same way. After the vocabulary came an ontology, first from The Open Group and then as an ISO standard (ISO/IEC 18384-3), which pinned down how services, contracts, and compositions relate. AOW is that step for agentic work.

## What an ontology adds

A glossary says what each word means. An ontology also says:

- **what each thing carries.** An Intent has a goal statement, a priority, an acceptable risk band, and a required Assurance Level.
- **how things connect.** An Intent serves an Objective, is shaped by Context, is constrained by Policy, and is realized by a Plan of Tasks.
- **what must be true.** Every Result was generated by an Action; every Action was performed by an Actor.

And because it's written down formally, software can check it. That's what turns "our agents are governed" from a claim into something an auditor can verify.

## What's different about agentic work

Services execute fixed operations. Agents interpret goals, plan, and act, with more or less autonomy depending on the stakes and on how sure they are. So an ontology of agentic work needs things SOA's never did:

- **Graded autonomy.** Not "automated or not," but five levels, from AL0 (people do the work) to AL4 (agents act without routine review), with a rule for what happens when an agent, a task, and a tool disagree.
- **Confidence.** The same agent should act alone when it's sure and escalate when it isn't.
- **People in the loop, on the record.** A person approving an agent's decision, or making it after an escalation, is part of the work and belongs in the same record.
- **Learning.** Outcomes and reviewers produce Feedback that changes Policies, Skills, and Memory, and that loop needs to be traceable too.

## What's still unsolved

AOW describes work inside one organization's governance. How autonomy, trust, and policy compose when agents from different organizations work together is open. So is the question of how to model negotiation between agents, and how to make confidence values trustworthy rather than merely present. The whitepaper's last sections list these, and they're where contributions would help most.
